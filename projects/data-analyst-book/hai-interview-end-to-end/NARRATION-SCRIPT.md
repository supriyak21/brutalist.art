## OPEN

**B00.** Supriya, for Humanitarians AI. Someone asked me a version of this question: what does a data analyst interview actually look like, start to finish? Most candidates prepare by collecting questions — a SQL site here, a stats sheet there. Here's the map instead: six stages, and each one tests something different.

## ACT I — The Six Stages

**B01.** Act one. The process, start to finish.

**B02.** A typical loop runs about five or six blocks: recruiter screen, hiring-manager chat, technical screen, statistics round, case round, behavioral round — roughly in that order.

**B03.** Most processes run three to five rounds over two to six weeks. Startups compress that to two or three rounds; larger, FAANG-adjacent companies often stretch it longer.

**B04.** Here's what separates good prep from hard prep: each stage scores something different. Learn that before you learn anything else.

## ACT II — What's Tested Where

**B05.** Act two. What's tested where.

**B06.** Recruiter screen: basic fit, communication, whether the logistics line up. Hiring-manager chat: relevant experience, and genuine motivation for this role.

**B07.** Technical screen: correct SQL or Python, and how clearly you explain your logic. Statistics round: sound reasoning under uncertainty, and stating your assumptions out loud.

**B08.** Case round: framing, assumptions, and a defensible recommendation — over raw correctness. Behavioral round: collaboration, ownership, and how you handle ambiguity.

**B09.** In the technical screen, a query that runs but that you can't explain is a weak answer. Interviewers grade logic and communication — not just whether the code executes.

**B10.** Prepping without this matrix is exactly how strong candidates give technically correct answers that still don't land.

## ACT III — What Changed by 2026

**B11.** Act three. What changed by 2026.

**B12.** First shift: AI-tool fluency is now assessed. You may be asked how you use tools like ChatGPT or Cursor responsibly.

**B13.** What they're listening for is judgment: when to lean on the tool, and when to read the generated query line by line — because AI output can look right and still be subtly wrong.

**B14.** Second shift: take-home case studies are common now. Mid-to-large companies send a real-feeling business question on intentionally messy data, with a time cap and a presentation after.

**B15.** Third shift: communication and business framing are weighted heavily. Later rounds reward stakeholder translation and judgment about which question is worth answering — not just the ability to compute.

**B16.** Put the map and the 2026 shifts together, and here's what a real three-week plan looks like.

## ACT IV — The Worked Plan

**B17.** Act four. The worked plan.

**B18.** Meet Maya. Economics degree, eight months out. Two statistics courses, one intro-databases course, a three-month analytics bootcamp — and interviews starting in three weeks.

**B19.** She can write a SELECT with a JOIN, and build a pivot table. She's never done a take-home case, or told a behavioral story that landed.

**B20.** Week one shores up the technical floor. SQL is the most-tested skill and she's slow at it, so SQL and the statistics round get ninety minutes a day.

**B21.** Week two covers what she's never done. Spreadsheets and pandas get light maintenance time — the real investment goes to the case round, including one full timed take-home.

**B22.** Week three integrates it all: behavioral stories, then the full mock loop, twice — self-scoring each stage and re-drilling the two weakest.

**B23.** Notice the plan is lopsided on purpose. Most of the hours go to the stages she's weakest at, and that count the most — not spread evenly across all six.

## CLOSE

**B_VERDICT.** Let's recap. Six stages, and each one scores something different — that matrix beats collecting random questions. Three to five rounds, two to six weeks. 2026 added three shifts: AI-tool judgment, messy-data take-homes, heavier weight on communication. A query you can't explain is a weak answer — screens grade logic, not just execution. And spend your prep time where you're weakest and the stage counts most: lopsided, on purpose.
